\section{Research Project}

Fuel cells are electrochemical energy conversion devices that directly transform chemical energy into electrical energy without the need for combustion processes, making them essential technologies for the global energy transition toward sustainable power generation systems \cite{Nigel2017}. Unlike conventional thermal devices, fuel cells rely on electrochemical reactions occurring at electrodes separated by an ion-conducting electrolyte, enabling high efficiency and low environmental impact \cite{Gurbinder2016}.

Fuel cells convert chemical energy directly into electrical energy through a redox reaction. Like thermal devices, they operate continuously as long as fuel and oxidant are supplied. Because of this nature, fuel cells are capable of continuously producing electricity with high conversion efficiency.

Several types of fuel cell technologies exist, each defined primarily by the electrolyte material and operating temperature range \cite{Gurbinder2016,skinnerd:2008}. Examples include polymer electrolyte membrane fuel cells, alkaline fuel cells, and solid oxide fuel cells. Despite differences in materials and temperature regimes, all fuel cell technologies share common characteristics such as high energy conversion efficiency, absence of moving mechanical parts, modular scalability, and low or near-zero pollutant emissions during operation.

Applications of fuel cells extend to a wide technological spectrum, from portable electronic devices and backup power systems to transportation and large-scale distributed electricity generation \cite{BinZhu2020}. These technologies contribute to reducing dependence on fossil fuels, lowering greenhouse gas emissions, improving energy security, and supporting the development of a hydrogen-based energy infrastructure.

Growing global concern regarding energy security, atmospheric pollution and greenhouse gas emissions has intensified research into environmentally sustainable energy technologies. Among the various fuel cell systems, Solid Oxide Fuel Cells (SOFC) have attracted considerable scientific and technological interest due to their high efficiency, fuel flexibility, and long-term stability \cite{Kevin2003}. SOFCs can operate using hydrogen, carbon monoxide, methane, and other hydrocarbon-based fuels, reducing the need for highly purified hydrogen and enabling integration with existing fuel infrastructures.

The project focuses on  symmetric Intermediate-Temperature Solid Oxide Fuel Cells \cite{JuanCarlos2011,KejunZhu2022,Muhammad2022,Liliana2016}, which operate typically between 500 and 700°C. Operating in this temperature range reduces thermal degradation, expands material choices, and lowers system costs while maintaining sufficient ionic conductivity and catalytic activity. Because of their symmetric configuration and reversible electrochemical behavior, these devices can also function as Solid Oxide Electrolyzer Cells (SOEC), enabling efficient electrolysis of water \cite{Meng2008}. When powered by renewable electricity, SOEC systems can produce green hydrogen, a key energy carrier in decarbonized energy systems.

As shown in Figure \ref{sofc}, a Solid Oxide Fuel Cell device is composed of a cathode, an anode, and a dense ceramic electrolyte. At the cathode, oxygen reduction reactions (ORR) convert molecular oxygen into oxide ions. Perovskite oxides such as lanthanum strontium manganite exhibit excellent electrocatalytic performance for this reaction \cite{Sacanell2017,Diego2017,JACOBSON2012478}. The anode promotes fuel oxidation (or Hydrogen Oxidation Reaction, HOR), commonly employing nickel yttria stabilized zirconia cermets capable of hydrogen oxidation and internal reforming of methane-containing fuels \cite{Nigel2017,skinnerd:2008}. The electrolyte is typically a dense yttria-stabilized zirconia ceramics, which provides high oxide-ion conductivity and chemical stability at elevated temperatures \cite{Nigel2017,skinnerd:2008}.

A major limitation of conventional SOFC technology is that peak efficiency occurs at temperatures near 900–1000$^\circ$C \cite{Nigel2017,Gurbinder2016,skinnerd:2008}. Such high temperatures restrict material selection for interconnects and structural components and accelerate degradation mechanisms such as thermal expansion mismatch, phase instability, and chemical inter diffusion. And materials such as lanthanum, strontium, and chromium are often required, which are costly. These challenges have motivated extensive research into materials capable of efficient operation at intermediate temperatures.

Despite significant advances in intermediate-temperature SOFC materials, many state-of-the-art cathodes still rely on elements such as lanthanum, cobalt, and cerium, which raise concerns regarding cost, supply risk, and environmental impact. In this context, this project proposes the exploration of alternative mixed ionic–electronic conductors (MIECs) based on earth-abundant and lower-cost elements, particularly iron- and molybdenum-based systems.

Initial candidate materials include perovskite-structured compounds such as \linebreak (La,Sr)(Fe,Mo)O$_{3-\delta}$, (Ba,Sr)(Fe,Mo)O$_{3-\delta}$, (La,Sm)(Fe)O$_{3-\delta}$, and (La,Sm)(Fe,Mo)O$_{3-\delta}$, which have demonstrated promising electrochemical performance in related studies \cite{GIL2024,MEJIAGOMEZ2020}. These systems benefit from tunable redox chemistry (Fe$^{3+}$/Fe$^{4+}$ and Mo$^{5+}$/Mo$^{6+}$), enhanced oxygen vacancy formation, and improved electronic conductivity, all of which are critical for oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) activity. In parallel, cobalt-free perovskites such as BaFeO$_{3-\delta}$ and SrFeO$_{3-\delta}$ will be investigated as cost-effective alternatives, leveraging Fe-based redox activity and high oxygen vacancy concentrations. Multi-component systems, including high-entropy oxides incorporating Fe, Mn, Ti, Cr, and Mo, will be considered for their enhanced structural stability, defect engineering capabilities, and resistance to degradation phenomena such as cation segregation and phase decomposition.

In addition to single-phase materials, composite cathodes based on ceria–perovskite combinations will be included as benchmark and advanced design strategies. In particular, samarium-doped ceria (SDC) combined with La$_{1-X}$Sr$_{X}$Co$_{1-X}$Fe$_{Y}$O$_{3-\delta}$ (LSCF) represents a well established system that integrates the high electronic conductivity and catalytic activity of LSCF with the superior ionic conductivity of ceria-based phases \cite{MARINHA2012,SHIMURA2017}. The incorporation of SDC extends oxygen ion transport beyond the conventional triple phase boundary (TPB), effectively enlarging the electrochemically active region and improving ORR kinetics, while reducing polarization resistance. Moreover, ceria-based components act as chemical buffers that suppress detrimental interfacial reactions with zirconia-based electrolytes, mitigating the formation of insulating phases and enhancing long-term stability, in agreement with the stabilizing role of ceria-based interlayers observed in advanced SOFC architectures \cite{KAN2016,TAO2009}.

From a microstructural perspective, SDC–LSCF composites promote improved porosity distribution, increased active surface area, and better thermal compatibility between cathode and electrolyte layers, making them particularly suitable for intermediate-temperature operation and durable cell architectures.

The inclusion of these material classes is technically justified by their potential to (i) enhance oxygen reduction kinetics through increased oxygen vacancy concentration and mobility, (ii) improve long-term stability by mitigating cation segregation and thermal mismatch, and (iii) reduce overall system cost through the use of abundant elements. Furthermore, their structural and electronic properties make them particularly suitable for investigation using advanced techniques such as in situ and operando X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAFS), enabling direct correlation between local atomic structure, redox behavior, and electrochemical performance.

In parallel, Solid Oxide Electrolyzer Cell (SOEC) systems will be developed using cobalt-free oxygen electrode materials based on (Ln,Sr)Fe$_{0.8}$Cu$_{0.2}$O$_{3-\delta}$ perovskite oxides, where Ln represents lanthanide elements such as La, Nd, Pr, or Gd. Among these, LaSFCu compositions have demonstrated excellent reversible performance in SOFC/SOEC operation. Composite materials incorporating ceria-based fluorites, such as LSFCu-CeO$_2$ nanocomposites with ceria contents, will also be investigated due to their enhanced oxygen transport and catalytic activity \cite{Natasha2026}.

Another topic proposed in this project is the valuation and development of nano catalysts materials for the steam reforming of ethanol (SRE). Bio-ethanol energy is a renewable energy resource, and its utilization can reduce pollution \cite{FENG2023}.  Bio-ethanol can be obtained from the fermentation of various raw materials containing sucrose, and cellulosic biomass \cite{Tripodi2018}. It also has high hydrogen content, non-toxicity, easy storage, and processing. Therefore, producing hydrogen from ethanol reforming process has gained widespread attention. The ethanol reforming process can be achieved by different oxidants \cite{FENG2023} including H$_2$O for the process called steam reforming of ethanol.

Although noble metals exhibit outstanding catalytic activity and stability, Ni-based catalysts remain the preferred choice due to their low cost and versatile catalytic properties, particularly for ethanol steam reforming (ESR). However, these systems face critical limitations, including carbon deposition, sintering, and sulfur poisoning, which lead to catalyst deactivation, structural degradation, and reduced lifetime \cite{SHI2025}.

To overcome these challenges, advanced strategies focus not only on the incorporation of metallic promoters but also on the rational design of catalyst supports, which play a decisive role in determining catalytic performance. In this context, both non-noble metals (e.g., Co, Cu, Fe, Mo) and noble metals (e.g., Rh, Pt, Pd, Ru) are combined with Ni to induce synergistic electronic and catalytic effects, enhancing resistance to coke formation and improving activity \cite{PHUNG2020}.

Equally important is the selection of supports such as CeO$_2$, ZrO$_2$, Al$_2$O$_3$, TiO$_2$, MgAl$_2$O$_4$, SiO$_2$, and mixed oxides (e.g., CeO$_2$ - ZrO$_2$). These materials provide distinct functionalities \cite{MONDAL2015}:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    %\begin{enumerate}
        \item Redox-active supports (CeO$_2$, CeO$_2$ - ZrO$_2$) enhance oxygen mobility and promote continuous carbon removal via oxygen vacancy dynamics \cite{Chirinos2025}.
        
        \item Acid–base supports (Al$_2$O$_3$, MgO, La$_2$O$_3$-modified systems) influence adsorption/desorption processes and suppress undesired reactions \cite{SUN2025}.

        \item Structurally stable supports (ZrO$_2$, MgAl$_2$O$_4$) improve thermal resistance and inhibit sintering \cite{TIAN2021}.

        \item Reducible supports (TiO$_2$) enable strong metal–support interactions (SMSI), affecting dispersion and catalytic pathways \cite{Sebastian2026}.

        \item High surface area supports ( SiO$_2$, SBA-15) enhance dispersion and accessibility of active sites \cite{KIM2015}.
    %\end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

These combined effects result in electronic modulation, geometric control, and oxidative protection mechanisms, which collectively improve catalyst stability, suppress coke formation, and enhance hydrogen selectivity.

Therefore, the selection of catalyst–support systems in this project is strategically designed to enable a multiscale understanding of structure–property relationships, correlating electronic structure, defect chemistry, and catalytic performance under realistic operating conditions.

In this context, the combined use of in situ/operando X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAFS) and Perturbed Angular Correlation (PAC) provides a powerful multiscale approach for catalyst characterization. While XAFS allows real-time monitoring of oxidation states and local coordination environments under operating conditions, PAC offers unique sensitivity to oxygen vacancies, local symmetry, and defect–dopant interactions. Together, these techniques enable a comprehensive understanding of the structure–property relationships governing catalytic activity, stability, and deactivation mechanisms in Ni-based systems.

The synthesized materials will undergo comprehensive characterization to determine structural, morphological, and redox properties. Techniques will include X-ray diffraction, and electron microscopy. Catalytic and electrocatalytic behavior will be evaluated using gas chromatography under controlled reaction conditions. These laboratory measurements will be performed at the CNPEM support facilities and will be complemented by in-situ and operando synchrotron based X-ray absorption spectroscopy experiments to directly observe structural and electronic changes during operation.

The research group possesses strong expertise in nanomaterial synthesis and preparation using multiple processing routes, including sol-gel synthesis, and combustion methods \cite{Diego2011,Lucia2016,Diego2024}. Those expertise ensures the feasibility of producing high-quality materials and overcoming synthesis challenges associated with complex perovskite systems and nanocomposite electrodes.

A central component of this project is the development of specialized experimental instrumentation compatible with synchrotron characterization. The project will leverage the capabilities of the QUATI beamline for nanoscale investigation of functional materials \cite{Santiago2023}. The methodology involves experimental engineering cells capable of operating under realistic catalytic and electrochemical conditions while allowing X-ray transmission and precise environmental control. These measurement cells will enable direct observation of catalytic reactions, electrocatalytic processes, and ionic transport phenomena in nanostructured materials during operation, establishing a powerful experimental framework for understanding and optimizing next-generation solid oxide electrochemical technologies.

In addition to synchrotron-based techniques, this project incorporates Perturbed Angular Correlation (PAC) spectroscopy as a complementary and highly sensitive nuclear method for probing local atomic environments at the sub-nanometric scale. PAC enables the investigation of hyperfine interactions through the measurement of electric field gradients (EFG) and magnetic hyperfine fields, providing unique information about local symmetry, charge distribution, and defect structures in functional materials \cite{Dogra2001,schatz_weidinger_1996}. The integration of PAC into this research framework is particularly relevant for the study of oxygen vacancies, dopant-defect interactions, and ionic mobility, which are critical factors governing the performance and durability of SOFC, SOEC, and catalytic systems. Using radioactive probe nuclei such as $^{111}$In($^{111}$Cd), introduced via thermal diffusion or implantation, PAC measurements allow the identification of distinct atomic-scale environments, including vacancy complexes and local distortions induced by aliovalent doping \cite{santos2024,silva2023,Ferreira2021}.

Within this project, PAC will be employed in a temperature-dependent and environment-controlled approach, allowing the investigation of defect dynamics and phase stability under conditions relevant to real device operation. When combined with synchrotron-based X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS), a powerful multiscale characterization framework emerges: while XAS provides element-specific electronic and structural information averaged over the material, PAC delivers site-specific insights into local disorder and defect configurations. This synergistic approach will enable the establishment of structure, property relationships across multiple length scales, bridging the gap between atomic-scale defects and macroscopic electrochemical performance. In particular, the correlation between oxygen vacancy behavior, redox processes, and catalytic activity will be explored in operando conditions, advancing the understanding of degradation mechanisms and performance limitations.
